WDO Inspection Basics: What Inspectors Look For

A WDO inspection is a structured hunt for five specific organisms and the conditions that invite them. Here is what Georgia's official report covers, the evidence an inspector is trained to find, and how to think about the job before you ever pick up a flashlight.

Five organisms — and only five

The first thing to understand about a Georgia WDO inspection is that its scope is set by rule, not by opinion. Georgia's official wood-destroying organism inspection report covers exactly five organisms under Rule 620-6-.03: subterranean termites, powder post beetles, wood boring beetles, drywood termites, and wood decaying fungi. That list is worth memorizing word for word. It defines what the inspection is for, what the report documents, and — for anyone studying — exactly what the WDO category of the Georgia Certified Operator exam can ask about. WDO is its own category on that exam, with a pass mark of 70%, and the five-organism list is one of its foundations.

Notice what the list does: it separates pests that behave very differently. Subterranean termites live in the soil and commute to the wood. Drywood termites live inside the wood itself. The two beetle groups attack wood in their own ways, and wood decaying fungi are not insects at all. An inspector who knows the list also knows to look for five different sets of signs.

The three things an inspector is looking for

Every WDO inspection comes down to three kinds of findings:

  • Live insects — the organisms themselves, caught in the act.
  • Evidence — the traces they leave behind: mud tubes, shed wings, frass, exit holes, and damaged wood that sounds hollow when tapped or probes soft when tested.
  • Conducive conditions — the problems that invite infestation even if no organism is found that day: wood-to-ground contact, moisture problems, cellulose debris under the structure, and inadequate ventilation.

Evidence matters as much as live insects because wood-destroying organisms are secretive. A colony can be active inside a wall or a sill plate and never show a single live insect during a visit. The frass, tubes and damaged wood are how the inspector reads a structure's history.

Reading the evidence, organism by organism

Subterranean termitesMud tubes built over foundation walls; shed wings; wood that sounds hollow or probes soft

Powder post beetlesFine frass and small round exit holes

Wood decaying fungiSoft, decayed wood, typically where moisture problems exist

Start with the subterraneans, the most familiar of the five. Because they live in the soil, they build mud tubes over foundation walls to travel between the ground and the wood above — a tube running up a foundation wall is one of the most recognizable findings in the trade. Higher up the checklist, drywood termites announce themselves differently: they push pellet-like frass out of the wood they live in, so small piles of pellets beneath furniture or woodwork deserve a close look. Powder post beetles leave fine frass and small round exit holes. Across all of them, the inspector's hands and ears do real work: damaged wood that sounds hollow when tapped, or probes soft when tested with a tool, tells you the inside of a member is gone even when the surface looks painted and sound.

Conducive conditions: the findings that predict the next infestation

A good inspection does not stop at organisms. The conditions that set up future damage get documented too, and the big four are easy to remember: wood-to-ground contact, which hands subterranean termites a hidden bridge into the structure; moisture problems, which soften wood and support decay fungi; cellulose debris under the structure — scrap wood and other food left in the crawl space; and inadequate ventilation, which lets that moisture linger. Reporting a conducive condition is not filler. It is often the most useful thing on the report, because it tells the customer what to fix before evidence becomes damage.

What the report does — and does not — say

Two limits define the inspection. First, it covers accessible areas — the parts of the structure an inspector can actually reach and see. Second, the report documents visible evidence at the time of inspection. It is a snapshot, not a guarantee about what is hidden inside finished walls or what will happen next year. Understanding those limits keeps inspectors honest and reports defensible: you document what you found, where you found it, and the conditions you observed, and the report says exactly that.

Common questions

Which organisms are on Georgia's official WDO inspection report?

Exactly five, under Rule 620-6-.03: subterranean termites, powder post beetles, wood boring beetles, drywood termites, and wood decaying fungi.

What evidence does a WDO inspector look for besides live insects?

Mud tubes, shed wings, frass, exit holes, and damaged wood that sounds hollow or probes soft — plus conducive conditions such as wood-to-ground contact, moisture problems, cellulose debris under the structure and inadequate ventilation.

How do drywood termites and powder post beetles show themselves differently?

Drywood termites push out pellet-like frass from the wood they live in. Powder post beetle evidence is fine frass together with small round exit holes.

Does a WDO report guarantee a structure is pest-free?

No. The inspection covers accessible areas and the report documents visible evidence at the time of inspection. It is a record of what was found that day, not a guarantee about hidden or future conditions.

Is WDO really its own exam category?

Yes. WDO is its own category on the Georgia Certified Operator exam, with a pass mark of 70%, and the five-organism list from Rule 620-6-.03 is core material for it.
