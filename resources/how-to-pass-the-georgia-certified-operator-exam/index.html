How to Pass the Georgia Certified Operator Exam

The Georgia Certified Operator exam is the top structural pest control credential in the state, and it is passable with a steady, category-by-category study plan. This guide covers what is on the test, the two skills candidates most often under-prepare for, and how to study around a working route.

What the exam covers

The Certified Operator exam tests four categories: General Knowledge, Household Pest Control (HPC), Wood-Destroying Organisms (WDO) and Georgia Regulations. General Knowledge covers the foundations every operator needs — pest biology and identification, pesticide safety, labels and application principles. HPC goes deeper into the pests and treatments of homes and businesses. WDO covers wood-destroying organisms and the inspections and reports that go with them. Georgia Regulations covers the state rules an operator is legally responsible for following. You need 70% to pass, and the exam fee is $45. Truman's 7th edition is the officially recommended study text, so it is the right reference to keep beside whatever practice material you use.

The two skills candidates under-prepare for

The operator exam does test label reading. Expect label excerpts with questions attached, where the answer has to come from what the excerpt actually says — the labeled rate, the use sites, the precautions — not from habit or from what a similar product allows. Practice reading a label section, then answering questions from it without looking back at your own assumptions. The exam also tests pesticide math: dilutions, application rates and area calculations. Work those problems the same way every time — find how much area one unit covers, scale to the job, and convert everything into the label's units before you compare. Candidates who only study pest facts and skip the label and math practice are leaving two whole question types unprepared.

Study category by category

Do not study the whole subject as one blur. Work through one category at a time — finish General Knowledge, then move to HPC, then WDO, then Georgia Regulations — and test yourself at the end of each block before moving on. Category-by-category study shows you exactly where you stand: a strong HPC score tells you nothing about your WDO readiness, and the exam will find the weak category even if you do not. Use practice questions with explanations, not questions alone. The explanation is where the learning happens: it tells you why the right answer is right and why the tempting wrong answers are wrong, so the next question on the same fact goes better.

Keep a missed-questions list and re-drill it

Every question you miss is a marked gap, so keep them. Keep a missed-questions list as you study, and re-drill that list regularly instead of only working fresh questions. A question you missed once and then answer correctly twice in a row, on different days, is a gap that has actually closed. A question you keep missing is telling you to go back to that topic in the study text, not just to memorize the answer. This single habit — collecting your misses and drilling them until they stay right — is worth more than re-reading chapters you already know.

Spend extra time on WDO and Georgia Regulations

Working technicians most often under-study WDO and Georgia Regulations. That makes sense on the job: day-to-day route work builds HPC and general knowledge constantly, while inspection rules, report requirements and state regulations may not come up every week. The exam does not grade on what your route taught you, though — it tests all four categories at the same standard. Deliberately give WDO and Georgia Regulations more study sessions than feels necessary, and test yourself on them more often, because familiarity from work will not carry those two the way it carries the others.

Take full timed mock exams before test day

Short quizzes and a full exam are different skills. Before test day, take full timed mock exams so you learn your own pace: how long a full run takes you, where you slow down, and whether label and math questions eat more of your time than fact questions. Mock exams also build the stamina to stay careful on the last questions, not just the first ones. If a mock shows a weak category, go back to category study and your missed-questions list, then take another full mock to confirm the fix.

Know the retake rule before you need it

Under Georgia Rule 620-3-.01, as amended effective April 8, 2026, the retake rule for certification is strict: two failures within 12 months means six months must elapse since the last exam before you can test again. That is a long wait, and it is one more reason to walk in prepared rather than treating the first attempt as a practice run. Study like the first attempt counts — because with a six-month penalty after a second failure, it does.
