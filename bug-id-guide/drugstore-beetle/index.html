Drugstore Beetle

Stegobium paniceum — a small brown pantry beetle, about 1/8 inch, keyed by two marks: lengthwise rows of pits striping its wing covers and a 3-segmented club on each antenna. It eats an astonishing range of dry goods — spices, medicines, books and leather included.

How to identify it

  • Size first: about 1/8 inch, oval and uniformly reddish-brown.
  • The stripes: the wing covers (elytra) carry distinct longitudinal rows of pits, giving a striped look under a lens — its twin, the cigarette beetle, has smooth, unstriated wing covers.
  • The antenna club: each antenna ends in a loose 3-segmented club; the cigarette beetle’s antennae are serrated (saw-toothed) instead.
  • Posture: the drugstore beetle sits flatter and more elongated; the cigarette beetle is humpbacked, head tucked down, and plays dead when disturbed.
  • Where you find it: in spices (famously paprika and chili), dry pet food, cereals, dried plants, books, leather and even pharmaceuticals — the "drugstore" in its name.

Signs you have them

  • Tiny round holes bored through packaging, book covers and spice containers.
  • Small brown beetles wandering windowsills and countertops near the pantry, drawn to light.
  • Fine powder and larvae inside opened spices, flour and dry pet food.
  • Damage to books, leather and dried plant material in storage — this beetle’s menu is wider than food alone.

The drugstore beetle earned its name because it is known to infest:

  1. Exclusively glass containers historically used to store liquid medicine formulations
  2. Only products containing significant amounts of refined sugar or sweeteners
  3. Only current prescription medications actively sitting on active pharmacy shelves
  4. A broad range of dried goods, including stored pharmaceuticals and dried herbs
Answer: A broad range of dried goods, including stored pharmaceuticals and dried herbs. The drugstore beetle has one of the broadest diets of any stored product pest, historically documented feeding on dried pharmaceuticals, dried herbs and spices, and book bindings among other dried goods — which is part of why finding one doesn't narrow down the likely infested product very much on its own.

How can a technician distinguish the cigarette beetle from the drugstore beetle?

  1. The drugstore beetle has serrated antennae, while the cigarette beetle has clubbed tips
  2. Cigarette beetle: serrated antennae, humped back; drugstore beetle: clubbed antennae
  3. They are identical species and cannot be told apart without DNA testing
  4. The cigarette beetle is roughly twice the size of the drugstore beetle
Answer: Cigarette beetle: serrated antennae, humped back; drugstore beetle: clubbed antennae. The cigarette beetle has serrate (saw-like) antennae and a humped back; the drugstore beetle has clubbed antennae with three enlarged end segments. Both infest dry stored products.

Drugstore Beetle questions, answered

Drugstore beetle vs cigarette beetle?

Wing covers and antennae: the drugstore beetle’s elytra are striped with rows of pits and its antennae end in a 3-segmented club. The cigarette beetle’s elytra are smooth, its antennae are saw-toothed, and its body looks humpbacked with the head tucked down.

How big is a drugstore beetle?

About 1/8 inch long — a small, oval, reddish-brown beetle, easily mistaken for its smooth-covered twin the cigarette beetle until you check the wing covers with a lens.

What do drugstore beetles eat?

Almost any dry organic material: flour and cereal, spices (a classic in paprika and chili powder), dried fruit, pet food, medicines, dried plants, book bindings and leather. Few stored-product pests have a wider menu.

Where do drugstore beetles come from?

Usually in with an infested product bought at the store; from there they spread through the pantry, boring out of one package and into the next. Control starts with finding and discarding the source items and storing replacements in sealed containers.
