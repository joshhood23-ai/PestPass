Bed Bug

Cimex lectularius — a flat, oval, reddish-brown, wingless insect about 1/4 inch long — apple-seed size. It feeds on blood at night, hides in mattress seams and furniture joints by day, and leaves its calling card: clusters of dark, ink-like fecal spots.

How to identify it

  • Size first: adults about 1/4 inch — an apple seed. Early nymphs are pinhead-size (1/16 inch), pale and translucent, with a red blood meal showing through after feeding.
  • Shape: flat and oval when unfed, like a tiny apple seed; after a blood meal the abdomen swells, elongates and turns deep red.
  • No wings, short antennae, and banded segments across the abdomen — never hairy, never laterally flattened like a flea.
  • Where you find it: mattress seams and tufts, box-spring frames, headboards, bed frames, then out into nightstands, baseboards and upholstered furniture as numbers grow.
  • Not a jumper or flier: bed bugs crawl. Anything that jumps is a flea; anything with wings is something else.

Signs you have them

  • Dark ink-like fecal spots clustered along mattress seams and on bed frames — each spot about 1/16 inch wide.
  • Shed skins (pale, hollow bug-shaped shells) in seams and joints as nymphs grow through five stages.
  • Rusty or blood-colored smears on sheets where fed bugs were crushed.
  • Bites in lines or clusters on exposed skin — suggestive, but bites alone never confirm bed bugs; find the insect or its signs.
  • In heavy infestations, a sweet, musty odor in the room.

HPC

Which describes a bed bug's body shape?

  1. An oval body with wings and pale yellow bands
  2. A narrow body with a distinct waist and four wings
  3. Oval, flattened top-to-bottom, reddish-brown, wingless
  4. Round-bodied with 6 legs, gray with distinct white stripes
Answer: Oval, flattened top-to-bottom, reddish-brown, wingless. Bed bugs are oval, dorsoventrally flattened, reddish-brown, wingless, 3/16 to 1/4 inch long. After a blood meal the body swells and becomes more reddish.
HPC

Primary bed bug harborage sites include:

  1. Mattress seams, box spring, headboard, cracks within 5-20 feet of the host
  2. Only within stored luggage and neatly folded clothing items in closets
  3. Deep within the soil found directly underneath the building's foundation slab
  4. Only inside wall voids and directly behind electrical outlet covers
Answer: Mattress seams, box spring, headboard, cracks within 5-20 feet of the host. Bed bugs prefer tight spaces close to the sleeping host — mattress seams, box spring, bed frame joints, headboard, and cracks in nearby walls and furniture.
HPC

Which confirms an active bed bug infestation besides live bugs?

  1. Bite patterns found on residents, treated as diagnostic evidence on their own
  2. Fine silk threads found woven into mattress seams from egg sacs
  3. Dark fecal spots, cast skins from molting nymphs, and cream-colored eggs
  4. A white, powdery residue found scattered across the mattress surface
Answer: Dark fecal spots, cast skins from molting nymphs, and cream-colored eggs. Active indicators: dark fecal spotting (digested blood), cast nymphal skins, cream-colored eggs (1mm) in harborage. Bite marks alone cannot diagnose bed bugs.

Bed Bug questions, answered

How big is a bed bug?

Adults are about 1/4 inch long — the size and shape of an apple seed. Nymphs start at about 1/16 inch (pinhead size) and pass through five stages, each requiring a blood meal, before reaching adult size.

Bed bug or carpet beetle larva?

Carpet beetle larvae are hairy, tapered "woolly bears" with banded segments — they look fuzzy, move slowly, and feed on wool and lint, not blood. A bed bug is smooth, flat and oval. If it is hairy, it is not a bed bug.

Do bed bugs spread disease?

Bed bugs are not known to transmit disease to people. The harm is the bites, lost sleep, anxiety, and the cost of elimination — plus how fast they spread between rooms and units through wall voids and on belongings.

Why do foggers fail on bed bugs?

Bed bugs hide deep in seams, joints and voids where fog cannot reach, and most are resistant to pyrethroids — the active ingredient in common foggers. Foggers mostly scatter them into new hiding places and neighboring rooms. Effective control combines inspection, heat (sustained 113–120°F) or targeted professional treatments, and interceptors for monitoring.
