Red Imported Fire Ant

Solenopsis invicta — the South’s notorious mound-builder. Workers are reddish-brown, 1/8 to 1/4 inch (sizes vary in the same colony), with two waist nodes and a painful, coordinated sting. Its fluffy, craterless soil mounds appear in lawns after rain.

How to identify it

  • Size first: workers range 1/8 to 1/4 inch within one colony — a mixed-size workforce is itself a fire ant clue.
  • Two nodes: a two-segmented waist, plus antennae of 10 segments ending in a distinct 2-segmented club.
  • Color: reddish-brown head and thorax with a darker abdomen.
  • The mound: loose, fluffy soil with no central entrance hole (native ants’ mounds usually have one), appearing in open sunny ground — lawns, fields, roadsides — especially after rain.
  • The response: disturb a mound and workers pour out and sting in unison, each leaving a white pustule a day later. That reaction alone separates fire ants from every other mound ant.

Signs you have them

  • Fluffy, craterless soil mounds in lawns, pastures, schoolyards and along fence lines, multiplying after rain.
  • Workers swarming up any object — a leg, a tool, a pet — that disturbs the mound, stinging in unison.
  • White pustules on skin a day after an encounter: the fire ant sting signature.
  • Colonies invading electrical equipment — AC units, traffic boxes, well pumps — a costly secondary habit.

The two-step fire ant IPM method involves:

  1. Spraying the entire property first, then following up with a granular product
  2. A bait-only program that never includes any individual mound treatment

Why is a bait-only fire ant program sometimes insufficient on its own?

  1. A bait-only program can take several weeks to show results, missing immediate problem spots
  2. Fire ants have developed complete biological immunity to every bait active ingredient available
  3. Bait products never work on fire ants under any circumstances, regardless of formulation
  4. Bait products carry significantly more health risk to applicators than contact sprays do

  1. Killing workers instantly on contact, before they can return to the mound
  2. Being carried back by foraging workers to eventually reach and affect the queen
  3. Dissolving the mound structure directly through a chemical reaction in the soil
  4. Repelling ants away from the treated area rather than actually killing the colony

Red Imported Fire Ant questions, answered

How do I identify a red imported fire ant?

Reddish-brown workers of mixed sizes (1/8 to 1/4 inch) with two waist nodes and a 2-segmented antennal club, pouring out of a fluffy soil mound with no central hole — and stinging in unison when disturbed. The sting response is the field confirmation.

Fire ant vs carpenter ant?

Fire ants are smaller (1/8–1/4 inch), reddish-brown, have two waist nodes, and sting. Carpenter ants are the big ones — 1/4 to 1/2 inch, black, one waist node, no sting, and they nest in wood, not soil mounds.

Are red imported fire ants established in Georgia and Florida?

Yes — the red imported fire ant (and its hybrid with the black imported fire ant in northern areas) is established across Florida and most of Georgia. It is the dominant mound-building stinging ant in both states.

What is the best way to treat fire ant mounds across a yard?

For many mounds, a broadcast bait that foragers carry back to the queens, supplemented by individual mound treatments where stings are a risk (play areas, schoolyards) — bait first, and do not disturb mounds before baiting, or the foragers will not feed normally.
