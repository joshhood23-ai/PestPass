Argentine Ant

Linepithema humile — a small 1/8-inch, light-brown ant that wins by numbers: its colonies hold many queens, merge into enormous supercolonies, and send endless trails into buildings. It does not sting — it overwhelms.

About 1/8 inWorker length
Light brownUniform body color
SupercoloniesHuge multi-queen colonies that merge instead of fighting
No stingA nuisance invader, not a stinging ant

How to identify it

  • Size first: about 1/8 inch, and all workers are the same size — no big-headed majors in the trail.
  • Color: uniformly light to medium brown, slightly darker at the abdomen tip. Darker, near-black small ants are odorous house or pavement ants instead.
  • One waist node, held low and inconspicuous, and no spines on the thorax.
  • Trails: wide, busy highways of ants along edges, pipes, wires and foundation lines — this species trails in numbers the other house ants never match.
  • No odor defense: crushed, it has no coconut smell (that is the odorous house ant) and it never stings.

Signs you have them

  • Thick, constant trails along the foundation, sidewalks and utility lines entering the building.
  • Ants swarming pet food, spilled sweets and ripe fruit — colonies have a strong sweet tooth in summer.
  • Colonies under mulch, stones, boards and leaf litter right against the structure, shifting indoors when it turns hot, dry or flooded.
  • Trails climbing trees to tend honeydew-producing insects (aphids and scale) in the canopy.

Which statement about the Argentine ant's colony structure is most accurate?

  1. Argentine ants only ever form small, single-queen colonies with no cooperation between nests
  2. Argentine colonies are often unicolonial, forming vast interconnected supercolonies
  3. Argentine ants cannot survive in any climate outside their native range at all
  4. Argentine ants are strictly solitary and do not form colonies of any kind
Answer: Argentine colonies are often unicolonial, forming vast interconnected supercolonies. Argentine ants are notable for unicolonial behavior — workers from different nests don’t treat each other as rivals, allowing enormous interconnected supercolonies to form, unlike most ant species where separate colonies compete aggressively.

Argentine Ant questions, answered

How do I identify an Argentine ant?

A uniform light-brown ant about 1/8 inch long, all workers one size, moving in thick trails. It has one waist node, no thorax spines, no sting, and no odor when crushed — the combination separates it from odorous house ants (darker, coconut odor) and pavement ants (thorax spines).

Why are Argentine ants so hard to control?

Their colonies have many queens and no colony boundaries — neighboring colonies merge into supercolonies instead of fighting. Killing foragers barely dents the population, and spraying repellents can scatter colonies into new areas; slow-acting baits that foragers carry home are the standard approach.

Do Argentine ants bite or sting?

No — Argentine ants do not sting and rarely bite people. They are a contamination and nuisance pest: the problem is sheer numbers in food, kitchens and electrical equipment.

Where are Argentine ants found?

Across the Southeast and Gulf Coast, California, and other warm regions — and in heated buildings farther north. They nest shallowly in soil, mulch and debris near structures and move with soil, plants and stored items.
