Black-legged Tick

Ixodes scapularis — the black-legged or "deer" tick. An unfed adult female is about 1/8 inch, with dark legs, a dark shield (scutum), and an orange-red abdomen with no white markings. It is the tick that transmits Lyme disease.

How to identify it

  • Size first: adults about 1/8 inch unfed; nymphs are poppy-seed size (about 1/16 inch) and easy to miss — and nymphs cause most Lyme infections.
  • Dark legs: the legs are black, contrasting with the body — the trait in the name.
  • The female: a dark shield covering just the front of the body, with an orange-red abdomen behind it and no white markings — a lone star female carries a single bold white spot; an American dog tick shows white mottling on its shield.
  • The male: smaller and uniformly dark, the shield covering the whole back.
  • Mouthparts: longer and more prominent than the stubby mouthparts of dog ticks — visible as a distinct "snout" from above.

Signs you have them

  • Ticks questing on the tips of grass, brush and leaf litter at ankle height along trails and yard edges.
  • Adults active in fall and early spring (and winter warm spells); poppy-seed nymphs peak in late spring and early summer.
  • Ticks on deer, mice, dogs and hikers after time in woods-edge and leaf-litter habitat.
  • An attached tick that swells from flat 1/8 inch to the size of a small pea over several days of feeding.

Black-legged Tick questions, answered

How do I identify a black-legged tick?

Dark (black) legs, a dark shield, and — on the female — an orange-red abdomen with no white markings, about 1/8 inch unfed. A single white spot means lone star tick; white mottling on the shield means American dog tick; uniform reddish-brown all over suggests brown dog tick.

Black-legged tick vs lone star tick?

The lone star female wears one bold white spot in the middle of her back and has rounder body shape and shorter mouthparts; the black-legged female has no white spot, dark legs, and longer mouthparts. Lone star ticks are also more aggressive, actively moving toward hosts.

Why are the nymphs such a concern?

Black-legged nymphs are poppy-seed size — about 1/16 inch — so they feed unnoticed for days, and they are the stage that transmits most Lyme disease infections. Adults are bigger, darker and easier to spot and remove quickly.

Where do black-legged ticks live?

In woods, woods-edge, brush and leaf litter — not in mowed lawns or trees. They wait ("quest") on vegetation tips for a passing host, and their life cycle runs through white-footed mice (nymphs) and deer (adults).
