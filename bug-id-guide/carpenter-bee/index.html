Carpenter Bee

Xylocopa species — a big, 1-inch bee that looks like a bumble bee until you check the rear: a carpenter bee’s abdomen is shiny, black and hairless, a bumble bee’s is fuzzy all over. It bores perfectly round 1/2-inch holes into decks, eaves and fences to nest.

How to identify it

  • Size first: about 1 inch — big and chunky like a bumble bee, far larger than a honey bee.
  • The abdomen: shiny, smooth and hairless black on top. This single mark separates it from the fully fuzzy bumble bee at a glance.
  • The hole: a perfectly round 1/2-inch entrance in bare or weathered wood — fascia, eaves, deck rails, fence posts — with yellow-brown frass and pollen stains streaking below.
  • Behavior: males hover and dart at passersby near the nest but cannot sting; females can sting but rarely do. They are solitary nesters, not a colony.
  • The tunnels: the entrance turns and runs with the grain, partitioned into brood cells; woodpeckers often tear the tunnels open to get at the larvae, multiplying the damage.

Signs you have them

  • Perfectly round 1/2-inch holes in fascia boards, eaves, porches, decks and wooden fences.
  • Coarse sawdust and yellow-brown stains on surfaces below the holes.
  • Big bees hovering noisily around the eaves in spring, males patrolling the nest entrances.
  • Woodpecker damage — long gouges ripped along the grain where birds hunted the larvae inside.

Why is carpenter bee damage described as cumulative over multiple years?

  1. Carpenter bees relocate to new wood every year, so damage never accumulates in any single location
  2. Generations reuse and extend the same gallery, growing one hole into a multi-foot tunnel network
  3. Carpenter bees only cause damage once, in their first year, before abandoning the site permanently
  4. New bees avoid old galleries, so each year's damage is always in fresh wood
Answer: Generations reuse and extend the same gallery, growing one hole into a multi-foot tunnel network. Because the same gallery gets reused and extended by successive generations, a single hole left unsealed can become an extensive tunnel network over several years — which is why sealing entry points after treatment matters.
WDO

Do carpenter bees eat the wood they tunnel into?

  1. Yes, wood is their primary food source, the same way it is for termites
  2. Yes, but only when tunneling into softwood tree species specifically
  3. No, they only ever tunnel into wood that is already decayed
  4. No — they excavate wood to create nesting galleries but do not eat it
Answer: No — they excavate wood to create nesting galleries but do not eat it. Unlike termites, carpenter bees do not eat wood — they excavate it purely to create nesting galleries where females lay eggs, which is an important distinction from wood-destroying organisms that actually consume wood as food.
WDO

Carpenter bee damage is most often found in:

  1. Brick veneer siding materials.
  2. Pressure-treated foundation walls.
  3. Unpainted, weathered exterior wood
  4. Steel framing structural components.
Answer: Unpainted, weathered exterior wood. Carpenter bees preferentially bore into unpainted, weathered, or softwood exterior surfaces such as eaves, fascia boards, and decks. Painted or treated wood, masonry, and steel are far more resistant to carpenter bee boring.

Carpenter Bee questions, answered

Carpenter bee or bumble bee?

Look at the abdomen: a carpenter bee’s is shiny, black and hairless on top; a bumble bee’s is covered in fuzz. Carpenter bees also hover around wooden structures and bore nest holes; bumble bees nest in the ground or in cavities with a social colony.

Do carpenter bees sting?

Males cannot sting at all — the hovering, dive-bombing guard at the nest is bluffing. Females have a sting but use it only when handled. Carpenter bees are a wood-damage problem, not a sting problem.

Do carpenter bees eat wood?

No. Like carpenter ants, they excavate wood for shelter, not food — adults feed on nectar and pollen and are legitimate pollinators. The damage is the tunnel system itself, worsened when woodpeckers open it up.

Why do carpenter bees come back to the same boards?

Females reuse and extend old tunnels year after year, and new bees are drawn to existing holes. Painting or finishing bare wood discourages new boring; weathered, unpainted softwood is the preferred target.
