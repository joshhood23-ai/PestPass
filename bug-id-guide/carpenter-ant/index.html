Carpenter Ant

Camponotus species — the biggest ant in the set, 1/4 to 1/2 inch, black (sometimes red-and-black), with one waist node and a smoothly rounded thorax. Carpenter ants do not eat wood — they excavate smooth galleries in damp, softened wood to live in, and their sawdust gives them away.

How to identify it

  • Size first: workers 1/4 to 1/2 inch — visibly the largest common house ant. Queens and swarmers are bigger still.
  • One node, round thorax: a single waist segment, and the thorax profile curves in one smooth, even arc with no dips or spines — the two marks that separate it from other large ants.
  • Color: usually solid black; some species and castes are black with a reddish thorax. Polymorphic workers come in several sizes in the same trail.
  • Where you find it: foraging mostly at night, indoors and out; nests in moisture-damaged wood — window frames, porches, decks, hollow doors, and tree stumps — with satellite colonies inside structures.
  • No sting: carpenter ants can bite but do not sting; a big black ant that stings is something else.

Signs you have them

  • Piles of coarse sawdust-like frass below woodwork — with dead ant body parts mixed in, the giveaway that separates it from termite debris.
  • Smooth, clean galleries inside damp wood, with no mud and no round exit holes.
  • Large black ants foraging indoors at night, trailing from a wall void or window frame.
  • Faint rustling sounds inside a wall or hollow door on quiet nights in an active nest.
  • Winged swarmers emerging indoors in spring from a colony inside the structure.

What is the FIRST step in treating a carpenter ant infestation?

Carpenter ant frass looks like:

  1. A fine white powder closely resembling talcum powder
  2. Sandy, gritty material closely resembling coarse garden soil
  3. Hard, hexagonal pellets featuring six clearly distinct sides
  4. Sawdust mixed with insect parts such as legs and antennae
Answer: Sawdust mixed with insect parts such as legs and antennae. Carpenter ant frass is distinctive: coarse sawdust-like shavings mixed with insect body parts. This differs from fine lyctid beetle frass and sandy drywood termite pellets.

What do carpenter ants eat?

  1. Wood cellulose fibers, digesting them the same way termites do
  2. Household fabric and stored paper products found indoors
  3. Structural lumber directly, using it for both food and shelter
  4. Proteins and sugars — they only excavate wood for nesting
Answer: Proteins and sugars — they only excavate wood for nesting. Carpenter ants do NOT eat wood. They excavate moisture-damaged wood for nesting galleries while foraging outside for proteins (insects) and sugars (honeydew, food).

Carpenter Ant questions, answered

Do carpenter ants eat wood?

No. Unlike termites, carpenter ants do not consume wood — they chew out smooth galleries in damp, softened wood purely as living space, and push the sawdust (frass) out. The frass contains wood shavings and dead ant body parts.

How do I tell carpenter ant damage from termite damage?

Carpenter ant galleries are smooth and clean, often following the grain, with coarse sawdust and insect parts piled below — and no mud. Subterranean termites pack their workings with soil and build mud tubes; drywood termites leave neat hexagonal pellets.

Carpenter ant or termite swarmer?

Ants have a pinched, one-node waist and elbowed antennae; termite swarmers have a broad waist (no pinch) and straight, bead-like antennae, with two pairs of equal-length wings. A carpenter ant swarmer is also much larger — up to about 3/4 inch with wings.

Where do carpenter ants nest?

A parent colony usually sits in a stump, log or other consistently moist wood outside; satellite colonies — workers and older larvae, no queen — move into wall voids, window frames and other damp wood in the structure. Fixing the moisture problem is part of any real solution.
